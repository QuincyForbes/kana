Four marks that unlock the rest

Modifiers

You only memorise 46 shapes. These four rules generate every other syllable in the language.

Dakuten ゛ — voicing

Two ticks top-right make the consonant voiced. No new shapes to learn.

か → がka → ga▸
さ → ざsa → za▸
た → だta → da▸
は → ばha → ba▸

Two oddballs: し→じ is ji, つ→づ is zu.

Handakuten ゜ — the P row

A small circle, and only ever on the は row.

は → ぱha → pa▸
ふ → ぷfu → pu▸
ほ → ぽho → po▸

Small ゃゅょ — glides

A shrunken ya/yu/yo after an i-row character fuses into one syllable. きや is two beats, きゃ is one.

きゃ きゅ きょkya kyu kyo▸
しゃ しゅ しょsha shu sho▸
ちゃ ちゅ ちょcha chu cho▸
じゃ じゅ じょja ju jo▸

Small っ and the bar ー

Small っ doubles the next consonant — it's a held beat of silence. きって → kitte, stamp.

In katakana, ー stretches the vowel: ラーメン → rāmen. Hiragana does the same job by just adding the vowel: おかあさん, とうきょう.

What actually makes you sound wrong

Pronunciation — read this before drilling

None of this is about the individual syllables. You'll say か correctly on your first attempt. Every one of these is a rule that operates between the kana, which is exactly what a chart of clips can't show you.

The big one

Every mora gets equal time

English is stress-timed — we compress unstressed syllables. Japanese gives every mora the same duration, including long vowels, small っ, and ん. Each is its own full beat.

とうきょう

Tokyo is four beats, not the two of English "TOH-kyo". Say it evenly and it clicks.

おばさん aunt · おばあさん grandmother
きて come · きって stamp
ここ here · こうこう high school

Length is meaning here, not emphasis. Getting it wrong doesn't sound like an accent — it's a different word.

The invisible one

い and う go silent

Between two voiceless consonants (k, s, t, h, p), or at the end of a word after one, those vowels devoice — the mouth shape happens, the voice doesn't.

です is "des", not "de-su". すき is "ski". 〜ました is "mash-ta". ひと comes out close to "shto".

This one rule accounts for more of the gap between textbook Japanese and actual Japanese than anything else on this page — and no kana chart anywhere will tell you, because it happens between the characters.

Vowels

Pure, short, and no gliding

English vowels drift mid-sound — "oh" ends in a w, "ay" ends in a y. Japanese vowels hold one position and stop.

う is the one to fix: it's unrounded. Don't purse your lips like English "oo" — say "ee" and then flatten your tongue back without moving your lips.

The ら row

Neither R nor L

It's an alveolar tap — the tongue touches the ridge behind your teeth once, quickly. You already own this sound: it's the dd in American "ladder" and the tt in "butter".

Say "ladder" and isolate that middle consonant. That's ら.

ん

A full beat that changes shape

ん is not the English n. It takes a whole mora of time, and it assimilates to whatever follows:

before p/b/m → m (しんぶん newspaper sounds like "shimbun")
before k/g → ng
before t/d/n → n
at the end of a word → a uvular sound made further back than any English consonant

You don't need to memorise this — it happens naturally if you give ん its full beat instead of rushing it.

ふ and つ

Two sounds English lacks

ふ isn't an f. Your teeth never touch your lip — it's made with both lips, like blowing out a candle gently.

つ exists in English only at the end of words: "cats", "boots". Say "cats", then drop everything before the ts. That's つ.

The advanced one

Pitch accent, not stress

Japanese distinguishes words by pitch pattern, not loudness. はし is chopsticks, bridge, or edge depending on where the pitch drops.

In Tokyo Japanese the first two mora always differ in pitch — never flat. Once a word's pitch drops, it stays down.

Don't drill this yet. But know it exists, so you're listening for it from the start rather than discovering it in a year and having to relearn vocabulary you've been saying flat.

Test your ear

Hear the difference

One of two words plays — length is the only difference. Tap what you heard. This is the skill the mora-timing rule above is describing.

Where to hear humans

Real voices, free

OJAD (Online Japanese Accent Dictionary, from Tokyo University of Foreign Studies) — pitch accent patterns with audio, for words and full conjugations. The single best free pronunciation resource.

Forvo — multiple native speakers per word, with their region listed. Every character in the chart above links straight to it.

YouGlish — search a word, hear it in real video clips in context, which is the only way to learn how it actually gets compressed in speech.

Shadowing — play a line, say it over the top, match the rhythm. Music you already know works well for this, since the timing is already in your head.

How to actually get through it

Two weeks, not two months
  1. Hiragana first, alone. Doing both scripts at once is how people stall for months. Katakana is much easier once hiragana is automatic, because half the shapes rhyme with it.
  2. Five rows a day, in order. あ / か / さ / た / な, then は / ま / や / ら / わ. Review yesterday's rows before adding today's.
  3. Write them by hand. Stroke order isn't pedantry — it's what makes the shape stick and what makes handwriting legible. Type the character into Google Images with 書き順 to see the order.
  4. Read, don't recite. Once you have a few rows, read real text slowly rather than flipping cards. Song titles and lyrics work well — you already know the sound, so you're only decoding the shapes.
  5. Drop romaji entirely by day three. Every extra day of reading romaji is a day your brain isn't building the mapping you need.
  6. Then katakana, same schedule. Practice on convenience-store shelves and menus — コーヒー, ビール, コンビニ. Nearly every katakana word is a loanword you already know.

One note on fonts: printed shapes differ slightly from handwritten ones — さ き ふ り are the usual culprits. What you see here is the gothic style used on Japanese signage, which is the version you'll be reading in the wild.
